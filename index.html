The Perplexity Intuition Guide

Theoretical Foundations

Everything you need to know about why language model evaluations use perplexity, why it uses the geometric mean, and what the scores actually mean in practice.

🎲 The Guessing Game Analogy

Imagine playing a game where you must guess the next word in a sentence. If an LLM has a perplexity of 10, it means on average across the sentence, the model is as uncertain as if it had to choose uniformly from a 10-sided die of equally likely words.

A lower perplexity means fewer effective choices, indicating the model found the text predictable and natural.

📐 Why Geometric Mean instead of Arithmetic?

The joint probability of an independent sequence is the product of token probabilities:
P(w₁, ..., w_N) = P(w₁) × P(w₂|w₁) × ... × P(w_N|w_{

Taking the \(N\)-th root (geometric mean) normalizes for sequence length! A 100-word sentence won't have an unfairly penalizing score compared to a 5-word sentence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the first token in Section 1 have a probability? ▼
To condition the first word, we prepend GPT-2's start-of-sequence delimiter token (<|endoftext|> ID 50256). The model then computes the probability of starting a text with that exact word.
Why is calculating perplexity via log-sum (\(\exp(\text{mean}(-\ln P))\)) mathematically equivalent? ▼
Multiplying many small numbers (e.g. \(0.001^{50}\)) causes floating point underflow to zero. Because \(\log(a \times b) = \log(a) + \log(b)\), summing \(-\ln(P_i)\) and dividing by \(N\) before taking \(\exp(\cdot)\) is numerically bulletproof while preserving exact mathematical identity.
Can perplexity ever be less than 1.0? ▼
No. For discrete probability distributions, the maximum probability of any token is 1.0. When \(P = 1.0\), surprisal \(1/P = 1.0\), giving a perplexity of exactly 1.0. Any uncertainty pushes probabilities below 1.0 and increases perplexity above 1.0.
